# Architecture

React/Vite provide menus and HUD. The Three.js gameplay bundle and versioned model bytes warm when the user creates or joins a room, before opening the room connection. Input, the first-person camera, the viewmodel, effects, audio and rendering run outside React; the HUD reads `HudFrame`s that `HudFeed` publishes through `HudStore` at up to 30 Hz. The local player's movement and weapon are predicted and corrected to authoritative state; opponents are drawn from buffered snapshots 100 ms behind the newest one.

This document describes the code in this checkout: the first-person game from the [first-person overhaul](superpowers/specs/2026-10-30-first-person-combat-overhaul-design.md). The [release record](RELEASE.md) identifies deployed revisions and separates automated, browser, real-input and deployment evidence; [/health](https://kpop.8westventures.com/health) is the canonical running build identity. The [Kannon Town v3](TOWN_V3.md), [operator](OPERATOR_ART.md) and [weapons](WEAPONS_ART.md) records document the current art and its limits. Physical-phone performance and two-household play remain separate acceptance gates.

One Node process serves static assets, the HTTP API and WebSocket matches. The simulation ticks on an exact 30 Hz schedule and sends snapshots at 15 Hz: each tick carries its scheduled time, so the engine sees exactly 1000/30 ms between ticks even when the event loop runs a little late (`TickSchedule` in `server/app.ts`; a loop more than three ticks behind, or a wall clock that jumps, starts the schedule afresh). Each connection receives its own snapshot (see [Snapshots](#snapshots-and-transport)). Rooms live in memory; profiles, crew membership, ratings and history persist in SQLite with WAL and foreign keys. Restarting ends live rooms but preserves committed data. This is a single-process design for an invited community, not horizontal scaling.

Earlier release records and fixtures (Sunbreak, the scout, Kannon Town v1 and v2) remain historical evidence for their own models and source revisions.

## Rules and networking

`shared/protocol.ts` defines the wire contract, `RULES` and `SCORE_POINTS`. `shared/weapons.ts` is the one table of weapons, perks, rewards, medals, explosives, the drone and loadout presets for the server, client prediction, bots and HUD. `shared/map.ts` holds the solid obstacles (including the eight checkpoint cover boxes), spawns and surface kinds. `shared/physics.ts` holds movement (walk, sprint, stamina-limited dash, crouch, slide, jump and vault), posture and eye heights, hit boxes, map rays and grenade trajectories, with a static 2 m grid over the obstacles for overlap and ray queries.

Clients send sequenced `InputFrame`s: movement, view yaw and pitch, held buttons (fire, aim, jump, sprint, dash, crouch, reload, knife, grenade), the weapon slot, a reward to use with an optional mortar target, and `viewTime`. They never send positions, damage or scores. `sanitizeInput` rejects frames with non-finite numbers, unknown reward ids or a mortar target outside the arena; `sanitizeLoadout` accepts only a complete loadout with one perk from each slot. The server applies fire cadence (an accumulator, so 30 Hz ticks keep each weapon's true rate without banking idle time), ammo and reserves, reloads (per shell for the Breaker 12), swap and raise times (× 0.75 with Quick Hands), sprint-to-fire delays, no firing before 70% of a vault, grenade cooking, knife timing, line of sight and spawn protection. The server queues each person's frames and simulates every frame once, in order, as one 1/30 s step with its own press edges, the same way the client predicts it. Once a player's frames arrive at about 30 a second, that player runs on a clock of their own: each frame runs one 1/30 s step after the frame before it (never later than the tick), so slide, vault and weapon timers follow the client's predicted clock, and each snapshot's `self.lastInputTime` tells the client which server time its acknowledged frame ran at (a silent player's idle frames leave it alone, so the client's count does not run ahead during a stall). A playout of up to two frames, which grows after a late frame and relaxes after five seconds without one, keeps other players' view of a jittery mover steady. A tick simulates at most two of one player's frames, within five frames (167 ms) of banked wall-clock time, so a backlog catches up but sending faster never moves anyone faster; more than six frames of backlog beyond the playout drops the oldest frame's movement but keeps its presses. With no frame waiting the body holds still, and after 300 ms without one the player brakes (and falls if airborne); a spawn, the round going live or a reconnect starts the player's clock afresh, and until the first frame after that has run, those idle frames leave the clock where it started, so that frame lines up with the page's count however long the round trip. A page that comes back after respawning while hidden therefore raises its weapon when it returns. Until frames stream (the first frames of a life, a sparse sender) they run as they come, on the tick's clock. A player's own timers (weapon raise, knife, reload and shells, grenade cook and fuse, slide end and cooldown, vault, fire cadence, sprint-to-fire, aim start) count that player's frames: when a stream starts, the clock check slews the clock or a clock far behind is put back on the tick, and for the first frame of a life, the timers move by the same amount (`shiftTimers`), so each one ends on the frame the client predicted. No timer gets further ahead of the frame clock, itself never later than the tick, than the 167 ms catch-up allowance. Moving later gives back only what the timers were using, so time saved while nothing runs buys nothing; a stream start moves them one frame back at most, whatever sequence number it carries; a frame forced through a full queue keeps real time; and off the stream, a reload that finishes on the tick moves the clock up to it, so no later frame shoots from behind it. The allowance comes back at 20 ms a second, so a connection that stalls every couple of seconds keeps its timers in step, and whole with each life, but not with a reconnect. That refill is also the most a modified page can keep gaining by how it sends frames: a random search on the real engine found at most 2.6% more shots a second than the weapon's rate. A frag held past its fuse goes off on the frame whose own clock reaches it; a later clock would lengthen that fuse, so one cook moves at most 167 ms with the clock, and a page sending four frames a second keeps a frag about 0.8 s past its fuse at most. Lag tolerance remains: a late frame runs on its own clock, up to 250 ms behind the tick plus the playout and any backlog behind it before that clock is put back on the tick, and a timer it starts ends that much sooner in server time. That is spent once and paid back by the next late frame, so it adds nothing to a rate of fire, and a modified page that holds frames back to use it stands still meanwhile. Bots hand over one frame per tick, stepped with the tick's own time. Stale input and disconnects clear actions.

`MovementPrediction` replays `movePlayer` for the local player from the latest `self` snapshot, starting at the server time its acknowledged frame ran at (`self.lastInputTime`), one 1/30 s step per sent input, with the server's movement context: rising edges against the previous accepted input, the equipped weapon's movement factors, and no sprint while cooking a grenade or knifing. The server steps each frame the same way, so replay matches it unless a long stall overflows the server's queue or a spike outlasts the catch-up allowance. Timers often end exactly on a frame (a 900 rpm gun fires every second frame, a slide lasts 24 frames), so both sides count a deadline as reached 0.01 ms before it (`reached` in `shared/physics.ts`), and `self` carries the player's own timers to the microsecond, including the ones only the server's runtime keeps (each slot's next shot, the last sprinting frame, when aim went down, a too-early press still held). `LocalWeapon` adopts them and replays its unacknowledged frames one 1/30 s step apart from `lastInputTime`, as the body does, so a frame the client mispredicted (a respawn, a stall, a slewed clock) is corrected at the next snapshot instead of carrying through a burst. `LocalPlayer` holds the predicted body and weapon and is fed by `GameView`, which draws the raise, reload and cook on its `weaponTime`: that clock counts the sent frames, so a snapshot that moves the timers moves it with them and nothing on screen jumps. A shell-by-shell reload on show keeps its look (how many shells, empty or not) and moves with the server's deadline, and frames sent during a countdown are never replayed, because the server refuses them. `tests/client/server-parity.test.ts` drives the real engine and the real `LocalPlayer`, through the real `InputPacer` and real snapshots, over steady, jittery and spiky links, a shorter route mid-run (the server slews the clock), a longer route at any moment of a run (the clock goes back on the tick), page stalls (the pacer skips frames), long uplink and downlink stalls, an uplink that stalls every few seconds for a minute, a respawn mid-burst on round trips up to half a second, the first frames of a round, a page sending through the countdown, and frags let go on the fuse's last frames. It checks that no snapshot moves the predicted body or changes the predicted weapon, reload look included, that the client predicts exactly the frames that fire or throw, and that the drawn raise never dips. One limit remains: until a new life's frames stream (about eight frames), jitter that brings two of them into one tick can put a timer a frame apart until the next snapshot; moving the timers there too would let a client that sends fast gain time. Confirmed shots carry the firing input frame (`seq`), which is how the Heron 7's server-drawn tracers are matched. After a reconnect the server counts inputs from zero again, and prediction takes the server state and replays none of the older frames. `LocalWeapon` mirrors cadence, ammo, reloads, swaps, sprint-to-fire and vault-fire rules so muzzle flash, recoil, sound, tracer and ammo count happen at once; the server's `shot` events confirm impacts and hit markers, and server ammo wins once acknowledged.

`LocomotionVelocity` measures each remote character's visible displacement after collision, prediction smoothing and interpolation. It changes only the velocity copy supplied to the animation controller. Opponent presentation uses the shared sprint ceiling while following its delayed position timeline. New-life protection, large teleports, dead or disconnected states, visibility changes and round resets discard presentation history. Neither this helper nor animation modifies authoritative snapshots or physics.

### Hits and lag compensation

There is no hip-fire bloom. Each shot is traced from the shooter's eye along the yaw and pitch the client sent, which already include recoil and view sway. Only the Heron 7 has a random cone: a 0.12 rad half-angle from the hip, narrowing to none as the scope comes up. The server rolls it, so the client draws that shot's tracer from the server's `shot` event. The Breaker 12 fires eight pellets in a fixed pattern scaled by its spread, which also narrows as the sights come up. Hit boxes are head, torso and legs per posture and do not turn with the player; legs take 0.85 of the damage (full damage for the Breaker 12, 0.67 for the Heron 7) and headshots use each weapon's multiplier. Damage falls off linearly between each weapon's near and far range and is summed per target and rounded.

Each input frame's `viewTime` is the server time of what the client was drawing. The server keeps one second of per-tick position and posture history for every player and traces opponents and drones where they were at `clamp(viewTime, now - RULES.lagCompensationMs, now)`; the knife uses the same rewind. `RULES.lagCompensationMs` is 400 ms. It was 200 ms until review showed that the client's 100 ms draw delay, the wait for the next tick and frame stamping used up most of 200 ms, so shots at moving targets stopped counting above about 100 ms round trip. 400 ms covers about 100 ms each way even on 30 Hz phone frames. The trade-off: a slow or dishonest client can hit a target up to 400 ms after it reached cover. Map collision is static and is never rewound.

The client lowers the weapon and holds fire while the wall straight ahead of the eye is closer than 0.55 m (released again at 0.65 m), so the eye-origin trace cannot shoot through cover the gun is pressed against. This is a client rule; the server trusts the eye trace. People's frames are fixed 1/30 s steps within the five-frame (167 ms) catch-up allowance, and bots and silent players step with the tick's elapsed time capped at 0.1 s against teleporting, so a long event-loop stall can slow movement while the match clock continues.

Within one simulation tick, all players finish movement and valid shots are accepted before any damage resolves. Mutual lethal hits can trade eliminations, and simultaneous score-limit hits can draw. If several attackers damage the same victim in one tick, the largest contribution receives elimination credit; equal contributions use a deterministic per-tick tie-break independent of join order.

### Health, explosives and rewards

Players spawn with 100 health, full magazines, the weapon table's spare magazines (four for most weapons, three loads for the Breaker 12; two more with Deep Pockets) and one frag (two with Deep Pockets). Health refills at 50 per second once 4 s have passed since the last damage (2.5 s with Second Wind). Spawn protection lasts 1.5 s (at a round start, until 1.5 s after the countdown), blocks damage and ends when the player fires, throws a grenade or uses the knife. Each elimination returns one magazine to the weapon used, up to the spawn amount.

A frag cooks while held and is thrown on release; it explodes 3.4 s after cooking started, in the hand if still held, and a player eliminated while cooking drops it live. Explosion damage needs a clear line from the blast to the target's torso. Your own frag can hurt you, your own mortar cannot, and teammates are never hurt.

Rewards come from eliminations in a row: Recon Sweep at 3 (enemies on the team's minimap for 20 s, except players with No Trace), Mortar Volley at 5 (a target inside the arena, then three shells, each scattered up to 2.5 m, at 2.0, 2.7 and 3.4 s on the highest surface under the point) and Attack Drone at 7 (circles the street for 25 s and fires every 450 ms at the nearest enemy it can see within 45 m, hitting more often up close; it has 200 health and can be shot down). Only gun, knife and frag eliminations extend a streak; reward kills do not. The streak resets on death, while unused rewards are kept (up to nine). Firing marks an enemy on your minimap for 2 s unless that enemy runs No Trace; teammates always show. Medals and score events use the names in `MEDAL_NAMES` and the points in `SCORE_POINTS`.

### Modes and spawns

Rooms are free-for-all (first to 15 eliminations) or Team Deathmatch (`create.mode: 'tdm'`, first team to 30); both end at five minutes. Ranked crew matches reject Team Deathmatch. In Team Deathmatch people and bots are balanced across Teal and Gold, bullets pass through teammates and nothing hurts a teammate. Spawn choice puts first the spawns no living enemy can see (an eye-to-chest ray), then unoccupied ones, then the farthest from enemies. In Team Deathmatch a round always starts on each team's own house side (Teal west, Gold east), and a respawn crosses to the far side only when enemies can see every home spawn and a far one is hidden. A round start spreads players over free spawns first, so teammates never stack. Every start and rematch rebalances the teams; during a round and while its results are on screen nobody changes sides, and a newcomer joins the side with fewer people.

### Snapshots and transport

`MatchEngine.snapshot(now, viewerId)` sends every player's public state (position, velocity, view, posture, health, team, loadout and weapon, scores and streak, reload and grenade-cooking state, aiming and sprinting) plus a full `self` for the viewer only. Other players' magazines, reserves, frags, unused rewards, stamina and weapon-ready timers stay private. Public positions are rounded to 3 decimals, angles to 4 and times to whole milliseconds; `self` keeps 6 decimals for prediction. Snapshots also carry drones, recon sweeps, mortars, team scores, the score limit and the mode. `app.ts` therefore builds one snapshot per connection.

Setting `WS_COMPRESSION=1` (exactly `1`; anything else, including `true`, leaves it off) turns on permessage-deflate for messages over 1 KB at the fastest level, with each socket keeping its compression context. An eight-player snapshot is about 5.5 KB uncompressed and under 0.5 KB compressed. It is off by default until it has been checked on real iPhones. `maxPayload` (4 KB) still bounds what a client message inflates to.

The browser detects eight seconds of silence while visible and retires stalled sockets without waiting for TCP closure. Coming back online retries automatically, up to eight times with backoff. Foreground return probes a quiet connection for up to 1.5 seconds before replacement, allowing a healthy connection to keep the host role. Authentication and session-replacement closures never trigger automatic takeover. Explicitly leaving retires the socket so delayed room messages cannot reopen the match.

Disconnected players have a 30-second recovery window. Movement, actions and published velocities clear on disconnect, and a grenade being cooked goes back in the pouch. A living player's body stays where it stopped and stays drawn: bullets, the knife, frags and the drone can still hit it, and its health refills only as an idle player's would, so dropping out neither dodges an elimination nor brings anyone back fresh. A body eliminated while its player is away respawns after the player returns. The first connected player becomes host when the former host is absent, including when an entire room returns from a connection loss. Rejoining a finished room replays its cached result explanation without writing ratings again; a rematch clears that cached result.

Host start or rematch first enters `preparing`. The fresh preparation ID equals `WorldSnapshot.roundId`; an authenticated `ready` acknowledgment must match it. Every person must be connected and ready before the full three-second countdown begins. The renderer proves a frame of that exact prepared world, then the player deliberately engages controls. Input blocking preserves mouse capture through the countdown while clearing held and queued actions; the server discards all inputs outside play. A 45-second timeout or an insufficient roster returns to the lobby without a result. Readiness is revoked on recovery and tab replacement. `hello.readyProtocol: 1` and a matching `hello.worldVersion` (now `kannon-town-v3`) gate older pages before start or rematch and cancel a preparation if an incompatible replacement joins. Input from an incompatible page is also rejected after reconnecting into a room already playing, so a page built for an older world cannot control a player in this one. See [Round readiness](ROUND_READINESS.md) for lifecycle details.

## Public lobbies and bot filling

Create messages accept `visibility: 'private' | 'public'`, `fillBots: boolean`, optional `botDifficulty: 'easy' | 'normal' | 'hard'` and optional `mode: 'ffa' | 'tdm'`. Omitted fields keep private, human-only free-for-all behaviour. The casual creation UI enables bot filling by default; hosts can turn it off. Ranked rooms must be private, free-for-all and without bots, and solo practice cannot be public. Invalid settings are rejected before leaving an existing room.

`GET /api/games` is a no-store, rate-limited public directory requiring no profile. Entries contain only a room ID, host nickname, mode, human and bot counts, maximum human capacity and bot settings. They never expose room invitation codes, crew invitations, keys or private rosters. Only opted-in, nonempty, waiting casual rooms with available human seats are included. Public joining uses an authenticated `join-public` WebSocket message and rechecks eligibility at admission; stale or full-room errors preserve the player's current room. Invite joining works separately.

The browser refreshes the directory every five seconds while visible and idle. Requests do not overlap; hidden pages and active entry suspend refreshing, and unmounted or superseded responses cannot replace current results. Loading, empty, failure and joining states remain distinct.

Casual bot filling keeps four lobby participants until four people join; more people can join up to the eight-person limit. Disconnected people's places count until their 30-second grace expires. Every match needs at least one connected person and two connected participants, and in Team Deathmatch someone connected on each team. Bots are implicitly ready, but every person must deliberately acknowledge preparation. Existing bots let the last connected person keep playing after a friend leaves. Bot membership stays fixed during live play; rematches rebuild the roster after pruning disconnected people. Solo practice stays private and restricted to its host.

## AI opponents

`server/bots.ts` generates ordinary input frames for three clearly labelled rivals. The engine applies the same movement, collision, weapons, ammo, cadence, health refill, protection, respawn and simultaneous-damage rules to people and bots. Bot-enabled matches include AI winners and time-limit or score-limit ties. Casual friends matches can use the same AI controller when the host enables filling.

Loadouts come from `botLoadout`: Scout carries Assault, Moxie Close Quarters, and Rook Assault (Marksman on Challenging). Bots perceive from their eye position, ignore teammates, aim at the torso (35% of aim decisions go for the head on Challenging) and pull down part of each recoil kick. They switch to the pistol when the primary is out of ammunition, or up close while the primary is empty or reloading; reload when empty or out of sight with a low magazine; knife within arm's reach; back off to cover below 45 health until they reach 90 or 12 s pass; throw frags at opponents who duck behind cover (15% of chances on Balanced, 30% on Challenging); dash on long quiet routes (Balanced and Challenging); sometimes slide into a fight (Challenging); and use their rewards, aiming the mortar at the freshest recent sighting of an enemy. In Team Deathmatch they share fresh sightings with bot teammates.

The optional `practiceDifficulty` create and room field accepts `easy`, `normal` or `hard`; omitted practice difficulty defaults to `normal`. The UI labels these Relaxed, Balanced and Challenging. Difficulty adjusts reaction delay, turning, aim error, aim lead, head aim, recoil control, perception range, burst and rest timing, engagement distance, frags, dashing and slides. It never grants extra health or damage. Invalid values are rejected before leaving the current room.

Perception samples visible opponents at 10 Hz with field-of-view and map-occlusion checks. Lost sight keeps a short last-seen position instead of tracking movement behind walls. `server/navigation.ts` builds and caches a layered visibility graph from the shared collision map and the explicit stair and access routes. It checks the union of supporting surfaces, rejects cross-height shortcuts and floor holes, and keeps each step transition; the eight checkpoint cover boxes are part of the map it reads. A visible upper opponent can prompt ascent; arrival waits until the landing is fully traversable. Recovery distinguishes real support from navigation padding and walks back without teleporting. Bots use ordinary movement and step-up physics; they never jump, so they do not vault or climb.

Bot actions stop when no person is connected, and recovery starts a fresh reaction delay; the match clock continues. Each rematch resets bot decision state with a fresh private seed. Real rooms roll the Heron 7's cone, drone hits and mortar scatter from a cryptographic source, because rolls derived from anything a client can see (the round id is in every snapshot) would let a modified page predict them. An internal `EngineOptions.practiceSeed` makes the rolls and the bots repeatable for tests and is never accepted over HTTP or WebSocket.

Practice is always unranked, including a forged create request combining `practice: true` with `ranked: true`. Requests combining ranked matches with public visibility, bot filling or Team Deathmatch are rejected. Bot identities, decisions and casual or solo results stay in room memory. No bot profile, rating, opponent-pair counter or match-history row is written.

## Room entry and asset lifetime

`src/game/assets.ts` keeps one shared `Promise<ArrayBuffer>` for each versioned model URL: the operator (`/models/operator-v1.glb?v=operator-v1`), the weapons (`/models/weapons-v1.glb?v=weapons-v1`) and the town (`/models/environment-v3.glb?v=kannon-town-v3`, or `environment-v3-phone.glb` on the phone tier). Warmup and the parsers consume these same bytes, avoiding a second model request after admission. Header length, GLB version, chunk boundaries and JSON asset version are checked before caching. GLTF parsing and GPU initialization still happen when the view starts; loading and error states keep gameplay input paused.

Each download owns a 60-second abort deadline, cleared when it settles. Failure evicts only that model's promise; a retry reloads its HTTP-cache entry so a corrupt cached response cannot poison later attempts. Successful model bytes remain for the page lifetime and later rooms reuse them. Callers read the buffers without mutation or transfer; cancelling one room-entry attempt does not abort a shared download needed by another consumer. The cache accepts only the configured asset URLs. The server sends `ETag` and `Last-Modified` with static files and answers a matching conditional request with 304, so a browser can keep its copy of a `no-cache` model and check it with a tiny request.

Room-entry operations carry a generation counter. Cancelling entry, leaving, replacing the player or unmounting invalidates pending work and acknowledgement timers. The captured player token is checked after preparation and again after connecting; a delayed profile-save response cannot replace a newer restored identity. Navigation and creation settings are disabled while preparing, and synchronous busy checks suppress repeated submissions. Closing the entry dialog remains available to cancel; it retires any pending room connection so late room messages cannot pull the player back in. Asset warmup may complete into its bounded cache after cancellation, but the cancelled operation cannot create or join a room.

An in-flight profile creation can finish after entry is cancelled. A newer nickname edit stays visible, and a subsequent entry waits for that profile, saves the new nickname on the same identity, then connects. Restoring a player invalidates older profile work, clears the previous crew list and leaderboard selection, and rejects delayed crew responses belonging to an earlier request or identity. The chosen loadout lives in browser settings. `LoadoutSync` sends it in a `loadout` message once the player settles on a choice (the server allows 20 changes in 10 seconds), straight away when the picker closes, and whenever the player joins a room or reconnects. The server applies it at the next spawn, or at once before the round is under way.

## Identity and privacy

A random opaque player key identifies a profile; only its hash is stored. The browser keeps the private key locally and authenticates HTTP with Bearer headers and WebSocket with its first message. Settings copies and restores it across devices. Nicknames do not establish identity. There is no email or password recovery, and sharing a key grants access to that player.

One WebSocket is active per player. A second replaces the first. Room invitations are random 80-bit codes with a two-hour life. Private crews use separate reusable random invitation capabilities; ranked participants and leaderboard viewers must be members. Member administration and invitation rotation are future work.

Controls include origin checks, payload and rate limits, room bounds, inactivity cleanup, slow-client backpressure and static file confinement. Each socket may send 90 messages a second on average and up to 320 at once, so a phone that delivers a stalled backlog keeps its match, while a flood closes the socket with code 1008. Rate limits are counted per client address and purpose: WebSocket upgrades (30 a minute), profile creation (20), crew changes (20), the public directory (180) and other API calls (180). By default the address is the TCP peer, and forwarding headers are ignored because any client can forge them. Behind a proxy every player then shares the proxy's address and one bucket, so `TRUSTED_CLIENT_IP_HEADER` names a header the proxy sets with the real player address, and the server counts limits by that address instead. It takes the header's first comma-separated value and falls back to the TCP peer when the header is missing or is not a valid IP address. Coastline's Compose override sets it to `cf-connecting-ip`, which Cloudflare writes on every request through the tunnel. Set it only when every request reaches the server through such a proxy; a client that can connect directly could otherwise choose its own bucket. Leave it unset when players connect to the server directly. Proxies must preserve Host and WebSocket upgrades.

These controls do not establish comprehensive anti-cheat. A modified client can automate aim, read the public positions it receives, create extra profiles, or use the 400 ms rewind to land shots slightly late. Public discovery lists only explicitly public casual rooms; automatic matchmaking is not implemented.

## Rankings

Crews have all-time and UTC-calendar-month stats. Ratings start at 1,000; five eligible matches place a player. Rating changes average pairwise Elo outcomes based on eliminations, K=32 and a 400-point expected-score scale. Tied eliminations draw the pairwise comparison. Rounded changes are stored; history shows all-time changes. A shared first-place draw counts as a match but not a win.

An eligible match needs at least one minute, two distinct crew members and a complete connected roster. Abandoned or incomplete games do not count. Each opponent pair can contribute five ranked matches per UTC day in that crew; if any pair exceeds the cap, the entire game remains unranked. Ranked matches are always free-for-all, so team results never reach ratings.

Results, both periods and opponent counters commit atomically. Unique match IDs prevent duplicate awards. Exclusions and save failures are reported rather than inventing successful results. The storage schema did not change in the first-person overhaul.

## Art and animation

Three Blender-generated models make up the game, each with its own generator, validator and record. The operator and the weapons are original geometry and textures; the operator's walk and run legs use the credited CMU capture described below. The town is original geometry dressed with Poly Haven CC0 textures and twenty Poly Haven CC0 props, listed in `art/source/town-v2/cc0-manifest.json` and named in each prop material's `provenance` extra:

- `operator-v1.glb`, the soldier other players see ([record](OPERATOR_ART.md)): the scout's 18-bone rig with unchanged bone names, the clips Idle, Walk, Run, Jump, Aim, Fire and Reload plus new Crouch, CrouchWalk and Slide, a `PlayerAccent` material on the armbands and patches for team or player colour, no weapon meshes, and an empty `weapon_socket` under `hand_r`. Poses are fitted to the shared hit boxes so a shot at what you see lands where the server checks.
- `weapons-v1.glb`, five firearms, the knife, the frag and two gloved arms ([record](WEAPONS_ART.md)). Root nodes keep their internal ids (`weapon_kr5`, `weapon_hornet9`, `weapon_breaker12`, `weapon_longbow` for the Heron 7, `weapon_sentry9`, `knife`, `frag`, `arm_left`, `arm_right`) with anchors for grips, muzzle, sight and ejection. `WeaponModels.ts` maps them, clones them per use and falls back to procedural stand-ins for any missing node; remote players hold the same models on `weapon_socket`.
- `environment-v3.glb` and its phone twin, [Kannon Town v3](TOWN_V3.md).

The operator's Walk and Run hips and legs are the scout's grounded capture sample for sample. That motion derives from the CMU `09_01` run capture, retargeted and refined in Blender (see [grounded gait](SCOUT_GROUNDED_GAIT.md)); geometry, surfaces, the upright upper body and the other clips are original. The selected skeleton and capture, source hashes and separate data terms are under `art/source/motion/cmu-09`, and `.gitattributes` marks those two publisher files as binary to keep their exact bytes. This motion is not CC0; credit and the restriction on direct resale of motion data accompany the [source](../art/source/motion/cmu-09/CMU-USAGE-NOTICE.md) and the [public model notice](../public/models/operator-motion-NOTICE.txt).

GLTFLoader and SkeletonUtils instantiate individual skinned players; the accent material distinguishes them (friends `#4aa3ff`, enemies `#ff5a4f` in Team Deathmatch, each player's colour in free-for-all). Runtime clip rate follows actual speed, including aimed and slow analog movement; a directional dead band avoids repeated gait reversal from joystick noise. The legs turn around world-up while the upper spine keeps its animated world orientation. Crouch, crouch walk and slide poses follow the published posture.

`CharacterBlend` owns base-pose weights independently of additive weapon actions. Gait transitions share cycle phase and phase rate throughout their 160 ms blend, and interrupted fades begin from all current action weights. During grounded blends, `LegContact` uses cached rigid boot vertices to find penetration and raises the ankle target, solving thigh and shin rotations while keeping foot orientation and segment lengths; it never moves the actor, pelvis or upper body. Fire and Reload are additive upper-body actions, with reload duration tied to the server timers.

The first-person view is separate. `FirstPersonCamera` places the eye at the predicted posture's eye height (1.62 m standing, 1.12 m crouched, 0.92 m sliding) with smoothing, landing dip, stride bob (off with Reduce camera motion) and slide roll, converts the horizontal field-of-view setting to vertical by aspect, and divides it by the weapon's zoom while aiming. `ViewModel` seats the arms on the weapon's grip anchors and layers procedural motion: look sway, stride bob, sprint and dash poses, crouch offset, slide tilt, jump and landing, aiming that aligns the `sight` anchor with the camera axis, recoil, reload choreography, raise and lower, the wall retract, grenade cook and throw, and the knife. It is hidden while the Heron 7 is fully scoped, when the HUD draws the scope instead.

## Rendering

Static lighting is baked. Kannon Town v3's lightmaps arrive through the project glTF extension `KANNON_lightmap`; `Lightmaps.ts` reads it as a `GLTFLoader` plugin and patches the standard shader for lightmapped materials so the real-time sun's direct term and the hemisphere fill are not counted twice. Each v3 lightmap carries a shade level in its alpha, so a character's shadow takes the baked light down to that level and no further. `Atmosphere.ts` holds the sun, hemisphere, fog and exposure the bake was made for, and a test fails if they drift apart. Characters keep the real-time sun, scaled by `SunVisibility.ts`, which casts a 64 × 64 grid of rays toward the sun on two layers (ground and upper floor) when the world loads.

`RenderTiers.ts` is the one table deciding what a device renders. The phone tier (touch devices on Automatic, or Low anywhere) loads the phone GLB, draws a 1024 character shadow map in a 14 m box that follows the local player, no grass cards, no post-processing, phone particle budgets, no muzzle lights and 48 bullet-hole decals. Desktop Automatic and High load the full GLB, a 2048 map in a 24 m box, grass cards, and, where floating-point targets are supported, the desktop presentation: contact shading, bloom above a high luminance threshold, aim-down-sights depth of field on the weapon only, the filmic grade with vignette and grain, and FXAA (Automatic) or SMAA with slight edge fringing (High). Phones get the same grade colour through a graded tone mapping and one cheap pass.

The viewmodel shares the world's field of view and is drawn over the finished world image with cleared depth, so the weapon never clips into walls. In the desktop presentation it is an overlay pass before bloom, blur, anti-aliasing and grade. `CombatEffects` draws muzzle flashes and smoke, tracers, surface impacts, bullet-hole decals in a ring buffer, ejected shells, grenades in flight, explosions, drones and the breakable glass and pots listed in `ArenaWorld.breakables`; all of it is cosmetic, driven by server events and the local player's predicted shots. Hits on players show dark puffs, never blood. `GameAudio` synthesizes every sound with Web Audio, with a 32-voice cap.

Graphics adapts scene resolution after about two seconds of slow frames; on a desktop, Automatic first turns the presentation off. Fifteen seconds of fast frames raise the resolution again in small steps, and time spent in another app does not count. Adaptive resolution changes are queued after quality evaluation and applied at the start of the next frame, before drawing, so resizing the drawing buffer never clears a completed frame before the browser presents it. Every texture is KTX2 and stays GPU-compressed; `Ktx2.ts` holds the one transcoder all loaders share, served from this origin.

## Touch and assisted firing

`TouchGestures` owns native TouchEvent identifiers independently for the move stick, look area, both Fire buttons, Jump, Reload, Grenade and Knife; Aim, Crouch and Sprint act on finger contact in hold or toggle mode, and Swap and reward buttons act once on contact. The document-level live-touch list reconciles missed element releases. Releasing movement preserves a separate aiming finger; stationary holds do not expire on a timer. In automatic sprint mode, pushing the stick (or holding W) past 95% forward sprints, sprint holds down to 75%, and a second push within 300 ms dashes. Lifecycle loss, death, mode changes and menus clear held and queued actions and reset the stick and button displays; late motion cannot resume controls without a fresh gesture. Background suspension resets frame timing rather than counting as sustained poor GPU performance.

Simple mobile firing requires the camera reticle, traced from the eye through the posture hit boxes, to rest on a living, visible, unprotected opponent for 100 ms, with a separate muzzle-to-target map check; teammates are never targets. Semi-automatic weapons are pulsed on alternate network frames so they fire once per press. It submits an ordinary input frame; the normal server combat code controls cadence, ammo, damage and hit confirmation. Advanced Fire can be dragged to aim. Optional gyroscope rates are projected through screen orientation, bounded and applied only to active input after the player enables sensor access.
